What makes a “good” book?

Analyzing Goodreads Users

Chapter 1: Do Ratings Reflect Reach?

Goodreads is built on the idea that a simple star rating can represent the value of a book, but when almost every book sits between 3.5 and 4.5 stars, the star system begins to lose meaning. When high scores become the default rather than a reward, the difference between good, great, and forgettable becomes almost impossible to see.

To dig deeper, we compared star ratings against two more grounded indicators of reader response:

  • Popularity – total number of ratings
  • Engagement – total number of written reviews

Use the visualizations on the right to toggle between “Popularity” and “Engagement”, and hover over each point to explore how individual books compare.

Popularity vs. Average Rating

The more people rate a book, the rating barely changes. Even massively popular titles stick within the same narrow star range as niche books. Popularity seems stable — but that stability isn’t necessarily honesty. It may reflect how easy it is to tap five stars and move on.

If a quick rating is simple, then stars reflect participation more than thoughtful opinion.

Engagement vs. Average Rating

Written reviews take effort — which means stronger feelings surface. Books with higher engagement show slightly more variation: some beloved hits rise, and a few truly controversial ones fall.

When people actually think before scoring, we finally see some disagreement. That suggests: Reviews reveal nuance. Ratings hide it.

So, is the star system broken?

Not completely — but it’s not telling the truth. The consistent inflation means stars can’t help you separate: a book you’ll genuinely love from a book you’ll forget tomorrow. These charts serve as a reminder: If every book looks “good,” how do you decide what’s actually worth reading?

Chapter 2: How Goodreads’ Growth Shaped Ratings

When looking at ratings alone, it's easy to assume that book quality determines visibility. But when we map publication year against engagement and popularity, a different pattern emerges:

Books released during Goodreads’ rise receive disproportionately higher visibility — regardless of critical or literary significance.

  • Publication Year vs. Engagement – total number of written reviews

Hover over each point to explore how books from different eras perform on Goodreads today.

Why Timing Matters

Goodreads launched in 2007, and its user base exploded between 2012–2016, largely driven by:

  • The John Green Era — YA fiction surge, Tumblr culture, and book fandom communities
  • Social reading becoming social media
  • Goodreads acquisition by Amazon, increasing reach
  • Smartphone adoption and app-enabled reviewing

Books aligned with these moments weren’t just read — they were shared, rated, and ritualized. Not necessarily because they were better, but because they circulated during the platform’s cultural peak.

Works from earlier decades — even classics — accrue slower engagement simply because:

  • They predate the platform
  • Their initial conversations happened offline
  • Their audiences may not be active Goodreads users
  • Their reputations were shaped academically, not socially

This introduces a temporal bias: quality is evaluated retroactively by the size of the audience that exists after the book was published.

Chapter 3: Top Rated Authors

So yeah, there's a bias towards newer released. However, that does not necessarily mean that the ratings are totally, irrevocably skewed towards newer releases. When looking at authors in our dataset who hold the most number of total reviews, there’s actually a decent mix of both pre and post goodreads popularity spike.

That being said, of the top 8 authors, the top 4 are all from the post-goodreads boom. Click the legend or the points on the graph to isolate certain authors' works.

While there isn’t a strong correlation between top rate authors and publication year, it is certainly interesting to note the sheer scale of certain book ratings. Take Twilight by Stephanie Meyer – even amongst the top 8 authors' works, it’s leagues above the rest. It makes you wonder – if goodreads existed during the 20th century, would a book like 1984 or Animal Farm be the number one?

Chapter 3: How does genre publication effect books?

When we look at the top 25 themes/genre publications from 1980–2025, we can see a clear popularity cycle emerge. This highlights a shift in both what readers' interests are and how the publication industry is responding to it. This suggests a structural bias: visibility strongly shapes engagement, and engagement, in turn, shapes what is perceived as a “good” book. Genres with large, active communities and a high count of yearly publications accumulate more ratings. Therefore, they appear “better,” while quieter or niche genres are overshadowed.

Genres like fantasy, adult fiction, romance, and mystery & crime spike in visibility during the 2000s and early 2010s, largely because they attract large, active reader communities. In contrast, categories such as world literature, classics, and religious & spiritual remain steady but far less represented. Furthermore, we can see a notable rise in LGBTQ themes after 2020, which suggests a shift in wanting more inclusive themes. These patterns suggest that Goodreads’ definition of a “good” book is shaped by community activity and cultural momentum more than the literary qualities of the works themselves.

Genre Legend

Epilogue:

Across our analysis, a clear pattern emerges: Goodreads tells us more about visibility and popularity than about intrinsic qualities shaping it as a ‘good’ book.

The star ratings cluster tightly between 3.5 and 4.5, devaluing the rating. Whereas written reviews, although fewer, show the real differences in reader opinion. The word clouds on this page highlight this divide even more clearly. Readers of highly rated books use language tied to emotional impact and enjoyment, while poorly rated books attract bigrams related to confusion, pacing issues, or disappointment.

Books published during Goodreads’ rise also receive far more engagement simply because they appeared at the right cultural moment. The same principle applies to authors and genres. Highly active communities generate the bulk of ratings, while smaller communities remain steady and overshadowed.

Together, these patterns suggest that Goodreads’ idea of a “good” book is shaped less by objective quality and more by timing, community behavior, and the ways readers choose to engage with the platform. Next time, you’re browsing through Goodreads to find your next book or see the ‘Goodreads’ award showcase, take a moment to consider these biases. Maybe even jump out of your comfort zone. Enjoy reading!
